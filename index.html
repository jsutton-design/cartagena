Getting Around+

Walk Getsemaní and the Walled City. Uber or InDrive otherwise; official yellow taxis late at night. Agree the fare before you get in — about 20,000–28,000 COP from the airport.

The planAppsFrom the airportGroup tips

The simple plan

  • Walk in Getsemaní and the Walled City during the day and early evening — Casa Marisol is right in the middle of it.
  • Uber or InDrive for most point-to-point rides.
  • Official yellow taxis as backup, especially late at night or for airport runs.
  • Skip public buses.

Heat, Rain & What to Wear+

High 80s, 78% humidity, and one heavy shower most afternoons that passes within the hour. Light clothes, flat shoes, something to cover your shoulders for churches.

August weatherRainWhat to wear

What August actually feels like

  • Highs around 88°F (31°C), nights around 80°F (27°C). Humidity sits near 78%, which pushes the heat index past 100°F in the afternoon.
  • The sea runs about 86°F — warm enough that swimming doesn't cool you down much.
  • Midday outdoors is draining rather than dangerous, but it adds up. San Felipe, La Popa and long walks are far better early morning or after 4 PM.
  • Carry water and refill at Casa Marisol rather than buying bottles all day. Something salty helps too — a full day of sweating takes out more than water.

Rain

  • August is wet season. Rain falls on roughly 24 days of the month, but almost always as one short heavy burst rather than all-day drizzle.
  • Showers tend to arrive mid to late afternoon and pass within the hour. Step into a café and wait rather than rearranging the day.
  • Streets flood quickly and drain slowly. Don't wade — you can't see the curbs, and the water is street runoff.
  • A folding poncho beats an umbrella in the wind and takes almost no space in a bag.

What to wear

  • Light cotton or linen. Synthetics are unpleasant by noon.
  • Churches expect covered shoulders and knees, including the cathedral in the Walled City and the Trinity church in Getsemaní. A light scarf in your bag solves it.
  • Getsemaní and the Walled City are cobblestone and uneven. Flat shoes with grip; leave the good ones at home.
  • The smarter restaurants and Bocagrande beach clubs don't want beachwear or flip-flops after dark.
  • A hat and sunglasses matter as much as sunscreen here — the sun sits close to overhead.
Rain rarely cancels anything in Cartagena. Boat trips are the exception — operators can delay or turn back for weather, so keep the day around a boat loose.

Where to be

  • Stick to the tourist core: Getsemaní, the Walled City, Bocagrande.
  • The streets around Plaza de la Trinidad stay busy and well lit into the night; the quieter side streets toward the edges of Getsemaní get sketchy late.

Basics

  • Don't walk empty or dim streets late at night — especially the edges of Getsemaní and toward Matuna.
  • Keep phones, cash, and jewelry low-key.
  • Don't leave bags unattended.
  • Order your car before you step outside a bar or restaurant.
  • Travel as a group after dinner or nightlife.
  • Confirm taxi fares up front if it's not app-based.

Going Out

Trouble happens in the transitions — leaving a bar, splitting off, heading back. Not in the middle of the night.
  • Pick a meeting point before you leave. Count heads before the group moves.
  • Don't follow a promoter or someone you just met to a second place.
  • Visitors to Casa Marisol show ID and get registered with the property. Don't hand out the address or door code.
  • Never leave a drink unattended, and don't accept an open one.
  • If someone robs you, hand it over.

Buying on the Street

  • Everything is for sale — hats, boat tours, food. "No, gracias," and keep walking; stopping is the yes.
  • Street performers open with "where are you from?", then follow you for blocks expecting a tip. Don't answer the first question.
  • Jet skis and beach rentals: agree the total price before you get on, on video or with a witness. Charging more on return is the standard scam.
Money & Payments+

Card in restaurants and larger shops, cash for taxis, street food and tips. Always pay in pesos, never dollars. Restaurants ask before adding a 10% tip and no is a normal answer.

Before you pay

  • Bring a card with no foreign transaction fee, and know which one it is before you fly.
  • Always accept the charge in local currency (COP), never USD — choosing USD lets the terminal set its own exchange rate.
  • Phone tap-to-pay (Apple Pay or Google Pay) is a backup, not primary — depends on the merchant having tap-to-pay.
  • Cash (COP) still essential for taxis, small shops, street vendors, tips.

ATMs

  • Easiest clusters: La Matuna and around Parque Centenario, a few minutes' walk from Casa Marisol. Also at the airport.
  • Always choose withdraw in pesos, never your home currency — avoid dynamic currency conversion.
  • Use ATMs inside a bank, mall, or the airport — Bancolombia, BBVA, Banco de Bogotá, Davivienda.
  • Make fewer, larger withdrawals — fees add up.
  • Use them during daylight; avoid isolated ATMs at night.
  • Ask for small bills. 50,000 and 100,000 notes are hard to break — taxis, street vendors and small shops often cannot change them, and pulling one out marks you as a tourist. Break big notes at supermarkets or restaurants and keep 10,000s and 20,000s for everything else.

Tipping

  • Sit-down restaurants add a 10% service charge, the propina voluntaria. By law the server has to ask before adding it — ¿Desea incluir el servicio? — and yes and no are both normal answers.
  • Check the bill before you answer. If servicio is already on the bill, you have tipped.
  • Street food, bakeries and juice carts: no tip, no service charge.
  • Taxis: no percentage. Round up to the next 500 or 1,000 pesos. Add a couple of thousand if the driver handles bags.
  • Housekeeping at the villa: 2,000–5,000 COP a day, left daily rather than in one lump at the end, so it reaches whoever actually cleaned.
  • Walking tours: 20,000–40,000 COP per person. A private full-day guide or boat crew: 10–15% of the cost.
  • Tip in pesos, not dollars — changing small US notes is a hassle nobody wants.
The 10% is shared among the staff, not kept by your server, and declining is normal if service was poor.

What to Eat+

Arepa de huevo, posta negra, ceviche and anything with coconut rice. The one to know about: arepa de huevo has a whole egg inside it.

Beef and pork

  • Posta negra cartagenera — the Cartagena dish. Beef slow-braised in a dark, glossy, slightly sweet panela sauce. Rich, tender, faintly molasses-like. Order this first.
  • Carne desmechada — shredded seasoned beef, often with rice and patacones.
  • Carimañola — torpedo-shaped fried yuca fritter stuffed with seasoned ground beef.
  • Empanadas de carne — corn dough, fried crisp.

Plantains and beans

  • Patacones — green plantains smashed flat and twice-fried, salty and crisp. Ask for suero costeño to dip.
  • Plátano maduro — ripe plantain fried until caramelized and sweet.
  • Arroz con coco — coconut rice, slightly sweet, often with raisins. The signature coastal side.
  • Cabeza de gato — mashed green plantain, a coastal comfort dish.
  • Frijoles cabecita negra — black-eyed pea stew, common on the coast.
Arepa de huevo is Cartagena's most famous street food, but it's a whole egg cracked inside and deep-fried — a runny yolk, not scrambled. Arepa de queso is the cheese-stuffed alternative if that's not for you.

Seafood

  • Mojarra frita — crisp whole fried fish, usually with arroz con coco and patacones. The classic coastal plate, bones and all.
  • Ceviche — Colombian style is often shrimp in a slightly creamy tomato-lime sauce, milder than Peruvian. Easy in small portions.
  • Filete de pescado — ask for filete rather than pescado frito, which comes whole with bones.
  • Cazuela de mariscos — coconut-milk seafood casserole. Rich and strongly seafood-forward.
  • Arroz de lisa — rice with salted mullet. More a Barranquilla dish than a Cartagena one, so order it if you spot it rather than hunting for it.

Soups

  • Mote de queso — thick, comforting soup of ñame (yam) and salty queso costeño. A Caribbean-coast specialty; worth it if you like cheese and hearty soups.

Snacks & breakfast

  • Butifarra soledeña — small seasoned pork sausages, served with lime. A popular coastal snack.
  • Chicharrón — fried pork belly or skin, often with arepa, yuca or plantain. Good for sharing.
  • Deditos de queso — fried dough sticks filled with melting salty cheese. The easy quick snack.
  • Buñuelos — warm fried cheese-and-yuca dough balls. Reliable at breakfast or any time.
  • Arepa de queso costeña — griddled corn arepa with salty coastal cheese worked through the dough, not stuffed inside.

Desserts

  • Torta de tres leches — with arequipe, coconut, or coffee.
  • Maracuyá (passion fruit) or guanábana (soursop).
  • Pie de mamey or pie de coco.
  • Enyucado — cassava and coconut, a regional specialty most visitors never try.
  • Cocadas from a street vendor — the arcades across from the Clock Tower, Portal de los Dulces, are the classic spot.

Handwoven crossbody bags made by Wayuu women of La Guajira. Each takes weeks to make, and the patterns carry family and clan meaning.

How to spot a real one

  • Check the weave density — hold it to the light. Single-thread bags are tight enough that little passes through.
  • Look at the strap (gaza) — genuine straps are handwoven with intricate patterning, not a plain machine-made band.
  • Slight irregularities are good. Perfectly uniform stitching means machine-made.
Many bags sold in Cartagena are locally made but not actually Wayuu. Ask directly and watch how they answer.

Every shop named here is in Places, with its address and a Maps button.

Sombrero vueltiao (woven hat)

Colombia's national hat, woven by the Zenú people from caña flecha palm. Quality is measured in vueltas — the number of fiber strands:

  • 15 vueltas — basic, stiff, tourist grade.
  • 19–21 vueltas — good quality.
  • 23–27 vueltas — fine. Soft and flexible enough to roll up and unroll without creasing.
Roll the brim in the shop. If it springs back cleanly, it's a good hat. If it creases, it's a cheap one.

What drives value

  • Colour is about 70% of the price. Vivid, saturated green; a slight blue cast is the classic Muzo look.
  • Clarity — emeralds are expected to have inclusions. Flawless at a low price means it is not an emerald.
  • Treatment — grades run none / minor / moderate / significant. It must be written on the invoice.
  • Carat matters least. A smaller, better stone beats a large, dull one.

Bringing it home

  • Buy loose, not mounted. Unset emeralds enter the US duty-free; set in metal it is jewelry and becomes dutiable.
  • Declare anything over the $800 personal exemption on CBP Form 6059B.
  • Pay by card — it is the only way to claim the tax refund.

How to buy without getting burned

  • Set a budget before you walk in, and visit two or three shops before deciding.
  • Get an itemised invoice: carat, origin, treatment grade.
  • Pay by card and keep every receipt — for the tax refund and for US customs.
  • Never buy from street or beach sellers. Almost every fake starts there.
  • A certificate is only as good as the lab. Ask which lab, and check it online before you pay.
  • For a significant purchase, get independent verification once you are home.
Cartagena is a tourist retail market with coastal markups, not a mining region — better for a memorable purchase than a bargain. Emeralds are illiquid and retail carries a steep markup, so buy one to wear, not to invest.

Beyond emeralds

Three other things are properly Colombian and travel better in a carry-on. All three are in Places.

  • Pre-Columbian gold reproductions — cast by the same lost-wax method as the originals in the Gold Museum. Galería Cano.
  • Mompox filigree (fine silver and gold threadwork) — a colonial craft, light and packable, far cheaper than emeralds. Joyas Momposinas L&L.
  • Caña flecha jewellery (woven palm fibre) — woven by Zenú artisans from the same fibre as the sombrero vueltiao. Artesanías de Colombia.

International Tax Refund (VAT/IVA)
International Tax Refund (VAT/IVA)+

About 16% back on things you can carry home — not meals, hotels or tours. Each receipt has to be over about $47, paid by card, with the invoice kept. Allow an extra hour at the airport.

Only physical goods you can show at the airport qualify — jewellery, crafts, clothing, leather. Anything used up in Colombia does not: meals, drinks, hotel nights, tours, taxis, fuel. Shops know which of their stock qualifies, so ask before you pay.

At the shop

  1. Pay by credit or debit card in your own name. Cash forfeits the refund entirely.
  2. Ask for a factura electrónica before you pay — a DIAN electronic invoice carrying your full name, passport number, a CUFE code and a QR square. A till receipt or handwritten one will not do.
  3. Print it, or ask the shop to. Check the QR and the numbers are sharp and uncropped before you leave — a scan that fails is a claim that fails.
  4. Keep the goods packed where you can reach them; you will be asked to show them.

At the airport

  1. Go to the DIAN desk before you check in. Some airports also have self-service kiosks.
  2. Bring your original passport with the entry stamp, plus a photocopy, the printed invoices, your boarding pass and the goods themselves.
  3. Fill in DIAN Form 1344 there, or ask for it in advance.
  4. The refund goes back to the card you paid with, usually within about a month.
  5. The total refund is capped around $2,990 per claim.

Embassy & Passport Help

Contact Tourist Police to report a lost or stolen passport first. There is no US consulate in Cartagena; the embassy in Bogotá handles all related matters.

Tourist Police — Cartagena+57 605 660 0583US Embassy Bogotá — American Citizens Services+57 601 275 2000US Embassy — after hours+57 1 275 4021US State Dept — from abroad, 24/7+1 202 501 4444

If your passport is lost or stolen:

  1. File a police report where it happened — the Tourist Police handle this for visitors.
  2. Email the police report plus the lost/stolen passport form to BogotaVisaPerdida@state.gov.
  3. Emergency passports are issued in person in Bogotá. Walk-in without an appointment Mon–Fri, 9:30–10:30 AM.
  4. Your passport photo is already on your phone, which speeds this up considerably.
Keep a passport photo on your phone — flat, edge-to-edge, no glare, every number readable. It makes replacing a lost passport much easier, and is handy for VAT refund shopping.
